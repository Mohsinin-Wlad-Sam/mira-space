Documentation

MIRA shows the sky changing: planets tonight, 27 years of NASA's all-sky pictures on one time bar, and stars caught changing place, each one measurable on NASA's SPHEREx exposures in your browser.

Sky map

The time bar

One bar carries four stretches: 1999 to 2010 (NASA's 2MASS survey fades into WISE), 2010 to 2025 (stars found moving appear in grey), 2025 to today (SPHEREx's picture is revealed patch by patch on the real dates of its exposures, and each moving star turns to colour once SPHEREx had imaged its place), and one year ahead (planets only). Live returns to now; My sky asks for your position, keeps it in the page, shades what is below your horizon and says what is up.

What the sky is made of SPHEREx only

SPHEREx is the first survey to record the whole sky in 102 colours of infrared light. MIRA uses that in three ways:

  • Composition pictures. Starlight (1.0–1.3 µm); carbon dust, as the 3.3 µm band of large carbon molecules with the light on either side subtracted; hot hydrogen, as the 4.05 µm line with its neighbours subtracted; and all three in one picture.
  • One wavelength at a time. The slider steps through the 102 wavelengths. The pictures are prepared ahead, so a step takes a fraction of a second.
  • Spectrum under the pointer. As you move over the map, the plot shows the spectrum of the 1.5° patch under the pointer, and says in words what it is made of. Click to pin a patch.

How far to trust it. These are built by census/sx_layers.py from the 8-bit preview frames of NASA's SPHEREx DR1 all-sky cube, decoded with the stretch stated in the cube's own description. They show what is where. Amounts are indicative: faint dust appears in coarse steps, and the brightest parts of the Galactic plane are clipped at 40 MJy/sr. For calibrated numbers on one object, use the Lab's Spectrograph, which reads the full exposures.

Survey depth and next visit for mission teams

Open SPHEREx, day by day and switch on Survey depth: every 1.8° patch is coloured by how many times SPHEREx has finished imaging it by the date shown. Move over the map to read, for the patch under the pointer, the dates of its visits and about when the next one is due (from the interval between its own past visits).

Markers

With the whole sky in view only the fastest movers are drawn; the rest appear as you zoom in. Zoomed in, a marker stands where its star was on the date shown, and for 1999 and 2010 the map draws the surveys' own sharp tiles from NASA's archive, so you can see the marker sit on the star. Click a marker for its card; click empty sky to watch that place through time, or to measure it in the Lab.

Space map

A 3D map of the Sun's neighbourhood from ESA's Gaia distances. The strip above the time bar steps through scales, from the planets to the nearest stars and beyond. Time has two regimes: among the planets it runs in days (the planets move on their real orbits); among the stars it runs in millennia (each star moves along its measured motion). Planets, live flies to the Solar System and starts the clock.

The Lab

The Lab opens on a picture, a time bar and four answers. Nothing else is on screen until you ask for it: click an answer to open the tool that produced it, or All tools for the list. Esc closes the drawer. A link to a field can carry the open tool (&tool=spectrum).

NASA's five test targets open at once: the site carries a prepared copy of each (rebuilt after every data release). Any other field is read live from NASA's archive, which takes 10 to 40 seconds the first time while the picture fills in, and is then kept in your browser, so opening it again is instant.

Search

Results appear as you type, from the planets, NASA's five test targets, landmarks, the 17,604 confirmed movers (by name, Gaia number or kind), 1,125 bright stars, 4,834 stars with planets, and MIRA's own tools and pages. It forgives spelling (“barnad”, “jupitr”, “epsilon indi” all work). If you do not know a name, open the filters and search by kind, motion and distance: for example every T dwarf within 65 light-years. Anything else is looked up in the CDS star databases when you press Enter.

Catalogue

From 39.9 billion WISE detections, 1.56 million candidate claims were made; 202,528 were re-measured on raw SPHEREx exposures; 17,604 motions were confirmed. 14,323 are known Gaia stars with a matching motion; 1,348 have no Gaia source at all. Each entry opens in the Lab with its evidence.

Assistant

The assistant reads what the page shows (never more) and can operate it: open a tool, fly to a star, play a date range. Its numbers are the instruments' numbers. It runs behind a small proxy, so no key is in the page.

Known limits

  • The pictures on the Sky map are not live. The date and the planets are. SPHEREx's picture stops at NASA's newest public release.
  • SPHEREx pixels are 6.15″. Motions below about 0.06″ per year over 27 years are not detected; close pairs blend.
  • Stars without a radial velocity are moved across the sky only, not toward or away from us, when time runs in 3D.
  • Constellation names on the Sky map are approximate near borders.
  • Composition pictures come from 8-bit previews: good for where things are, not for amounts.
  • The assistant takes 15 to 45 seconds for a long explanation.

Sky world model research preview

A world model is a network that, shown what happened, predicts what happens next. MIRA's is small and specific: it is shown two real pictures of the same patch of sky taken about eleven years apart (2MASS 1999, WISE 2010) and draws a later one. To do that it has to learn the one rule this sky obeys on human time scales: a star keeps the motion it has.

Loading the model's test figures…

  • Trained on our university's GPU cluster on real pictures from NASA's archive, with artificial moving stars painted in (real fast movers are rare). Code: census/wm_data.py, census/wm_train.py.
  • Tested only on real, Gaia-confirmed fast movers of MIRA's catalogue, in pictures it never saw, with nothing painted in.
  • Runs in your browser on the pictures the Lab already holds. The site needs no GPU and no server for it.
  • Marked as what it is. Every picture the network draws carries the label “AI-generated · prediction”. It is a forecast, not a measurement: the Motion detector and the Astrometer measure.

Where this goes: the same recipe scales to a model that predicts each new SPHEREx visit from the earlier ones and flags only what it did not expect. That is the search a ninth planet needs: a faint source that is in no catalogue and is not where anything was before.

New data releases

One script, deploy/update_spherex.sh, rebuilds the exposure index, the coverage dates and the composition pictures from NASA's archive. The site itself reads exposures live, so a new release shows in the Lab as soon as the index is rebuilt. web/scripts/prebake_fields.mjs then prepares the five test fields again.
