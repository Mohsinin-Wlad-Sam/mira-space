Documentation

MIRA shows the sky changing: planets tonight, 27 years of NASA's all-sky pictures on one time bar, and stars caught changing place, each one measurable on NASA's SPHEREx exposures in your browser.

Open the SkyOpen the Lab on a star that moved

Start here

There are four places, and one search box that reaches all of them.

PlaceWhat you do thereFor
SkyThe whole sky on one time bar, 1999 to today and a year ahead. Planets live, your own horizon, SPHEREx's survey day by day, what the sky is made of.everyone
SpaceFly from the planets to the nearest stars in 3D, and run time: days among the planets, millennia among the stars.everyone

Press / anywhere to search. Press Ask to have the assistant explain what is on screen or operate it for you.

Quick start

Nothing to install. Three minutes:

  1. Open the Sky. /explore/#sky. Drag the time bar at the bottom from 1999 to today.
  2. Click any place, then Watch this place through time: three NASA surveys of that spot, as a little film.
  3. Measure it in the Lab. The inspector on the right answers four questions: what moved, how fast, what its light says, did it change. Click an answer to open the tool that produced it.
  4. Search with /, or press Ask and let the assistant explain what is on screen.

Or start from a star that moved: Epsilon Indi B, Barnard's Star, WISE 0855.

Sky map

The time bar

One bar carries four stretches: 1999 to 2010 (NASA's 2MASS survey fades into WISE), 2010 to 2025 (stars found moving appear in grey), 2025 to today (SPHEREx's picture is revealed patch by patch on the real dates of its exposures, and each moving star turns to colour once SPHEREx had imaged its place), and one year ahead (planets only). Live returns to now; My sky asks for your position, keeps it in the page, shades what is below your horizon and says what is up.

What the sky is made of SPHEREx only

SPHEREx is the first survey to record the whole sky in 102 colours of infrared light. MIRA uses that in three ways:

  • Composition pictures. Starlight (1.0–1.3 µm); carbon dust, as the 3.3 µm band of large carbon molecules with the light on either side subtracted; hot hydrogen, as the 4.05 µm line with its neighbours subtracted; and all three in one picture.
  • One wavelength at a time. The slider steps through the 102 wavelengths. The pictures are prepared ahead, so a step takes a fraction of a second.
  • Spectrum under the pointer. As you move over the map, the plot shows the spectrum of the 1.5° patch under the pointer, and says in words what it is made of. Click to pin a patch.

How far to trust it. These are built by census/sx_layers.py from the 8-bit preview frames of NASA's SPHEREx DR1 all-sky cube, decoded with the stretch stated in the cube's own description. They show what is where. Amounts are indicative: faint dust appears in coarse steps, and the brightest parts of the Galactic plane are clipped at 40 MJy/sr. For calibrated numbers on one object, use the Lab's Spectrograph, which reads the full exposures.

Survey depth and next visit for mission teams

Open SPHEREx, day by day and switch on Survey depth: every 1.8° patch is coloured by how many times SPHEREx has finished imaging it by the date shown. Move over the map to read, for the patch under the pointer, the dates of its visits and about when the next one is due (from the interval between its own past visits).

Markers

With the whole sky in view only the fastest movers are drawn; the rest appear as you zoom in. Zoomed in, a marker stands where its star was on the date shown, and the map draws the surveys' own tiles from NASA's archive (2MASS for 1999, WISE for 2010, SPHEREx's all-sky cube at 4.6 µm for today), placed by their sky coordinates, so you can see the marker sit on the star. Click a marker for its card; click empty sky to watch that place through time, or to measure it in the Lab.

Why the closest view shows squares. SPHEREx's all-sky picture has pixels 13″ wide (its camera's are 6″). Enlarged beyond that, MIRA shows those pixels as they are instead of blurring them into detail that is not there, and stops zooming at 2.6″ per screen pixel, where no all-sky survey used here has more to show. For the full exposures of one place, open it in the Lab.

Space map

A 3D map of the Sun's neighbourhood from ESA's Gaia distances. The strip above the time bar steps through scales, from the planets to the nearest stars and beyond. Time has two regimes: among the planets it runs in days (the planets move on their real orbits); among the stars it runs in millennia (each star moves along its measured motion). Planets, live flies to the Solar System and starts the clock.

The Lab

The Lab is a workspace laid out like an editing tool. The picture is in the centre and never moves. Left: a rail with one icon per tool; the button at its top shows the tools with their names, in groups (Picture, Measure, Researcher). Right: the inspector, which shows the four readings (what moved, how fast, what its light says, did it change) or the tool you opened. Top: the target, the view (Evolution, Exposures, Colour, Difference) and the field of view. Bottom: the timeline with its dated pictures, and a status line with the data in use and the position and value under the pointer.

Working on the picture. Scroll on it to enlarge it about the pointer (up to 8×), drag to move, double-click to reset: positions and values under the pointer stay exact. In the Picture tool, the brightness stretch brings out faint sources without touching the data. Esc hides the inspector; PRO at the foot of the rail adds the researcher tools (the fit, the limits, every exposure, exports, watchlists, the sky model); a link to one of them switches them on. A link to a field can carry the open tool (&tool=spectrum).

Search

Results appear as you type, from the planets, NASA's five test targets, landmarks, the 17,604 confirmed movers (by name, Gaia number or kind), 1,125 bright stars, 4,834 stars with planets, and MIRA's own tools and pages. It forgives spelling (“barnad”, “jupitr”, “epsilon indi” all work). If you do not know a name, open the filters and search by kind, motion and distance: for example every T dwarf within 65 light-years. Anything else is looked up in the CDS star databases when you press Enter.

Catalogue

From 39.9 billion WISE detections, 1.56 million candidate claims were made; 202,528 were re-measured on raw SPHEREx exposures; 17,604 motions were confirmed. 14,323 are known Gaia stars with a matching motion; 1,348 have no Gaia source at all. Each entry opens in the Lab with its evidence.

Assistant

The assistant reads what the page shows (never more) and can operate it: open a tool, fly to a star, play a date range. Its numbers are the instruments' numbers. It runs behind a small proxy, so no key is in the page.

Known limits

  • The pictures on the Sky map are not live. The date and the planets are. SPHEREx's picture stops at NASA's newest public release.
  • SPHEREx pixels are 6.15″. Motions below about 0.06″ per year over 27 years are not detected; close pairs blend.
  • Stars without a radial velocity are moved across the sky only, not toward or away from us, when time runs in 3D.
  • Constellation names on the Sky map are approximate near borders.
  • Composition pictures come from 8-bit previews: good for where things are, not for amounts.
  • The assistant takes 15 to 45 seconds for a long explanation.

API

Everything MIRA measured and indexed can be asked for by a program. The API is served by the same host under /api/v1; it answers in JSON, and the list endpoints also in CSV (format=csv). Positions are ICRS degrees, motions arcseconds a year. No key on this site; 300 calls per ten minutes per address.

Examples

# every confirmed T dwarf that Gaia does not list, as a table
curl "https://YOUR-HOST/api/v1/movers?kind=T%20dwarf&gaia=absent&format=csv"

# which SPHEREx exposures hold Barnard's Star, detector 5 only
curl "https://YOUR-HOST/api/v1/exposures?ra=269.452&dec=4.693&detector=5"

# a list of candidates against the catalogue, the survey and the sky model
curl -X POST "https://YOUR-HOST/api/v1/crossmatch" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
     -d '{"positions":[{"id":"a","ra":331.0966,"dec":-56.8007},{"id":"b","ra":10,"dec":10}],"radius_arcsec":30}'
# Python: the movers around a position as an Astropy table, then one exposure's cutout
import requests, astropy.units as u
from astropy.table import Table
from astropy.io import fits
from astropy.wcs import WCS
from astropy.nddata import Cutout2D
from astropy.coordinates import SkyCoord

HOST = "https://YOUR-HOST"
movers = Table(requests.get(f"{HOST}/api/v1/movers", params={"ra": 331.1, "dec": -56.8, "radius": 2}).json()["movers"])
print(movers["id", "motion_arcsec_yr", "kind", "gaia"])

exp = requests.get(f"{HOST}/api/v1/exposures", params={"ra": 331.0966, "dec": -56.8007, "detector": 5}).json()["exposures"][0]
with fits.open(exp["url"], use_fsspec=True) as hdul:          # reads only the part of the 65 MB file it needs
    wcs = WCS(hdul["IMAGE"].header)
    cut = Cutout2D(hdul["IMAGE"].section, SkyCoord(331.0966, -56.8007, unit="deg"), 3 * u.arcmin, wcs=wcs)

Errors are JSON too, { "error": "…" }, with status 400 (a parameter), 401 (a key is asked for), 404, 429 (too many calls) or 503 (the server does not carry that file). Private deployments can ask for a key: set MIRA_API_KEYS and callers send X-API-Key. A purely static copy of the site has no API: the same answers are in the data files. Planned for the Mission plan: POST /measure, the Lab's whole chain on a position, run on the server (the engine already runs without a browser in our verification pipeline), and a call-back at each data release.

New data releases

One script, deploy/update_spherex.sh, rebuilds the exposure index, the coverage dates and the composition pictures from NASA's archive. The site itself reads exposures live, so a new release shows in the Lab as soon as the index is rebuilt. web/scripts/prebake_fields.mjs then prepares the five test fields again.

  • Change on a branch; npm run build must pass. Keep the words on screen plain, give every number its unit and its source, and add no dependency that a few lines can replace.
  • Claims need a check on real data that the claim did not come from: see how the detector was tested blind (Accuracy).
  • AI tools are welcome and logged: add a row to docs/07_si_usage_log.md saying what was made and how it was checked.
  • Licence. The team has not chosen one yet; until it does, ask before reusing the code. NASA's and ESA's data keep their own terms (Credits).

FAQ

QuestionAnswer
Is MIRA part of NASA?No. It is a NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026 project that reads NASA's public data. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by NASA.
Do I need an account?No. Nothing about you is stored on a server; your cases and settings stay in your browser.
Why does a new field take 10 to 40 seconds?Each SPHEREx exposure is a 65 MB file on NASA's archive. MIRA reads only the part it needs from each, in your browser. The second time the field is instant.
Is a mark of the sky model a discovery?No. It is a place to look at. Of the 400 strongest, 362 are fast-moving stars that Gaia or SIMBAD already list; 38 are in neither and stay open until measured.
Why are some tools behind PRO?They are the tools a researcher needs and a visitor rarely does. The switch is free while MIRA is a prototype.
Which browsers?Current Chrome, Edge, Safari and Firefox. The Universe needs WebGL; everything else works without it.
Can it find Planet Nine?Not by itself. The Detection limits tool shows that Planet Nine as proposed is far too faint for one SPHEREx field; MIRA makes the search for nearer, warmer bodies something anyone can follow.
